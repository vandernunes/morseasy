      How to run this

      • Two sessions of 10 minutes beats one of 30. Every day matters more than how long.
      • Never write down dots and dashes. Write the letter or nothing.
      • Miss one? Let it go and stay with the next character. Chasing a miss costs you the next three.
      • Leave character speed at 18 wpm or higher. Speed up the gaps, never the characters.

      What the separators mean

      • BT (=) — a pause, like a comma or a new paragraph.
      • KN — over, to you only. Others should not break in.
      • K — over, anyone may answer.
      • AR — end of this transmission, no reply expected yet.
      • SK — end of contact. The last thing you send.
      • 8 dits — I made an error, here comes that word again.

      Using a real key or paddle instead

      Your rig is a better trainer than this page

      • Plug the paddle into KEY on the back of the radio.
      • On an IC-7300: SET → Function → set Break-In to OFF.
      • Set Side Tone Level to taste and the pitch to match this page.
      • The paddle now drives the internal keyer and sidetone with no RF out until you press TRANSMIT. Real feel, zero chance of keying up the band.
      • Set the internal keyer speed to match what you are copying. Never send faster than you can receive.

      A paddle plugged straight into a computer

      • A key or paddle is a passive switch. A computer headset jack expects a microphone signal, not a contact closure, so a plain 3.5 mm cable produces nothing usable.
      • What does work: a USB keyer interface that presents the paddle as a keyboard or serial device, or the rig method above.
      • Until then the pads and keyboard here give correct timing and correct decoding. What they cannot teach is muscle memory in your hand.
      About hardware CW decoders. Keep one for curiosity, not for learning. They fall apart on hand-sent CW, fading and interference — exactly the signals you need to copy — and reading a screen trains your eyes while your ears stay untrained. Use one to check your own sending, never to copy someone else.

      Where to find slow CW on the air

      • SKCC straight-key calling frequencies: 7.055, 14.050, 3.550. Patient operators, mechanical keys, happy to slow down.
      • K1USN SST — a one-hour slow-speed sprint, 20 wpm max, Fridays and Sundays. The friendliest first contest that exists.
      • CWops CWT — Wednesdays, fast, but you can copy just the callsigns as practice without transmitting.
      • W1AW code practice — scheduled slow-speed transmissions on several bands, sent by the ARRL.
      • POTA CW activations sit just above the band edges, usually 13–18 wpm with a short fixed exchange.
      • General class CW segments: 3.525–3.600, 7.025–7.125, 14.025–14.150, 21.025–21.200, 28.000–28.300.

      Structured help, mostly free

      • CW Academy (CWops) — semester-based classes with a live advisor, beginner through advanced. The single most effective thing on this list.
      • Long Island CW Club — daily Zoom classes, small annual fee, very beginner-friendly.
      • SKCC and FISTS — membership clubs with sked pages where you can arrange a slow, forgiving first contact with someone who expects a beginner.
      • lcwo.net and Morse Code Ninja — more drilling than any one person can finish.

      How this works

      1Never count dots. Characters are always sent fast so you learn each one as a single sound. The gaps between them are what make it slow enough to follow.
      2Every lesson runs in three steps. First you just listen to the new sound. Then you name one character at a time and it tells you the answer every time. Only then do you copy groups of five.
      3Type or tap. Use your keyboard, or the on-screen keys — whichever is in front of you. Space replays, Esc stops.
      4Never guess. If a sound will not come back to you, tap it in Hear any character below the practice box. Looking it up teaches you something; guessing teaches you nothing.
      5Ten minutes, twice a day. Beats one long session every time. Come back tomorrow and the day after.
      Your callsign — optional Drops your own call into the practice contacts and the callsign drill. You can add it any time under the gear — and you do not need a licence to learn the code.
      Too fast? Tap Crawl in the speed bar at the top. Too slow? Tap Steady. Everything else lives under the gear.
